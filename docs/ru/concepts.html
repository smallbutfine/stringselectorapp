What Is String Tension?

String tension is the pulling force a string exerts on the instrument while it is tuned to pitch. Every string on your guitar is stretched between two fixed points — the bridge and the nut (or the fret you are holding) — and the amount of stretch is what we hear as pitch. The harder the string is pulled, the higher the note and the tighter it feels under your fingers.

Tension matters for three reasons that every tech and luthier deals with daily:

Playability

High-tension strings feel stiff, fight your fretting hand, and cause fatigue. Low-tension strings feel sloppy, buzz easily, and can rattle against frets. The right tension is the difference between a guitar that plays itself and one that fights back.

Setup & Intonation

Tension changes string stiffness and elasticity, which shifts intonation, action, and neck relief. A setup done at one tension will drift when you change to a different set or tuning.

Structural Load

A set of six strings can pull with 100–180 lbs of combined force. That load is constantly bending the neck, loading the bridge, and stressing the headstock. Knowing the total is essential before you adjust a truss rod or swap to a heavier set.

Rule of thumb: A typical electric guitar set (10–46) at standard tuning on a 25.5" scale pulls roughly 105–110 lbs total. A bass set can exceed 150 lbs. These are not small numbers — your neck is engineered around them.

The Tension Formula

Every tension number StringSelectorApp produces comes from one equation — the ideal flexible-string formula, solved for pounds-force:

T = (W × (2 × L × f)2) / 386.4

It looks intimidating, but each term is something you already understand. Here it is in plain language:

Unit Weight vs. Gauge

This is the single most misunderstood concept in string tension, and the one that causes the most bad setups. Gauge is not tension. Gauge is just the diameter of the string. Tension depends on how much the string weighs per inch — its unit weight.

Why ".010" is not enough information

A .010" plain steel string and a .010" nickel-wound string of the same gauge can differ by 20% or more in tension. The diameter is identical, but the mass is not. Here is why:

FactorEffect on unit weightEffect on tension
MaterialSteel, nickel-plated steel, stainless steel, and bronze all have different densities. Stainless is denser than plain steel; phosphor bronze is denser than nickel.Same gauge, different material = different tension. A stainless .010 pulls harder than a nickel-plated .010.
WindingWound strings have a core wire wrapped with a thinner outer wire. The wrap adds mass without adding much diameter. Roundwound, flatwound, and tapewound all change the mass.Flatwounds typically have higher unit weight than roundwounds of the same gauge — they feel stiffer and pull harder.
Core shapeHex cores vs. round cores change how tightly the wrap seats, which changes the effective mass.Hex-core strings often have slightly higher tension for the same gauge.
Manufacturing toleranceTwo manufacturers' ".010" can differ by a few thousandths of an inch in actual diameter.Small gauge differences compound across a set.

Never guess unit weight from gauge alone. StringSelectorApp stores the measured or published unit weight for every string in every pack — that is the value the formula uses. If you are adding your own strings to strings.ini, use the manufacturer's published unit weight or measure it yourself (weigh a known length on a precision scale and divide). Do not estimate it from the gauge.
How to measure unit weight yourself: Cut a 10-inch length of string, weigh it in grams on a jeweler's scale, then convert: W (lbs/in) = grams ÷ 453.592 ÷ 10. A .046 nickel-wound low E should come out around 0.00038 lbs/in. If your measurement is way off, your scale is not precise enough or the string is not what you think it is.

Scale Length

Scale length is the vibrating length of the string — the distance from the bridge saddle to the nut. It is the second most important factor in tension after unit weight, and it is the one most players never think about until they switch guitars.

Why 25.5" vs. 24.75" matters

The two most common electric guitar scale lengths are Fender-style 25.5" and Gibson-style 24.75". That 0.75" difference sounds tiny, but because tension scales with the square of scale length, the effect is roughly 10%:

(25.5 / 24.75)2 = 1.061  →  ~6% more tension on the longer scale

In practice, the perceived difference is closer to 8–10% because of how string stiffness and elasticity interact with scale. The shorter Gibson scale feels slinkier and easier to bend; the longer Fender scale feels tighter, has more snap, and stays in tune better under heavy bending. Neither is "right" — they are different tools.

Multiscale / fanned-fret instruments

Multiscale instruments (also called fanned-fret) use a different scale length for each string. The bass strings are longer, the treble strings are shorter, and the frets fan out across the fretboard. A typical 6-string multiscale might run 25.5" on the high E and 26.5" on the low E.

The reason is tension balance: on a single-scale guitar, the low strings always feel floppier than the high strings because they are thicker but tuned lower. By making the bass strings longer, a multiscale instrument evens out the tension across the set — the low strings get tighter, the high strings stay the same, and the whole set feels more uniform under your fingers.

In StringSelectorApp: Set the Treble Scale and Bass Scale fields to your instrument's values. The app interpolates linearly across the strings — string 1 uses the treble scale, the lowest string uses the bass scale, and everything in between is calculated proportionally. For a single-scale instrument, set both fields to the same value.

Tuning and Frequency

The formula needs a frequency in Hz, but you give it a note name like E2 or Bb3. The app converts one to the other using equal temperament — the tuning system that divides the octave into 12 equal semitones.

How note names become frequencies

Equal temperament is built on one reference: A4 = 440 Hz (concert pitch). Every other note is a fixed number of semitones away from A4, and each semitone is a frequency ratio of the 12th root of 2 (approximately 1.0595). The formula is:

f = A4 × 2(n / 12)

where n is the number of semitones from A4 (positive = above, negative = below). A few examples:

Transposition

Transposition shifts every string up or down by the same interval — for example, tuning the whole guitar up a half-step (Eb Standard) or down a whole step (D Standard). In the app, the Transpose field does this for you: enter +1 for up a half-step, -2 for down a whole step, and every note in the tuning is shifted before the frequency is calculated.

Concert pitch is adjustable. The app defaults to A4 = 440 Hz, but you can change it (390–460 Hz) in the GUI. This matters for period instruments, orchestral work, or if you tune to a reference that is not 440. A 432 Hz reference, for example, lowers every frequency by about 32 cents — and lowers tension slightly with it.
Tension follows frequency. Tuning up raises frequency, which raises tension — and tuning down lowers both. A whole-step drop in tuning reduces tension by roughly 12% across the set. That is why drop-tuned guitars often need heavier strings to keep the tension where it feels right.

Balanced Tension

"Balanced tension" means the strings in a set pull with a consistent feel across the fretboard — not that they all have the same tension number. This is a subtle but crucial distinction.

Why "balanced" is not "equal"

If every string had the same tension in pounds, the set would feel wrong. The low strings would feel too tight and the high strings too loose, because your fingers perceive tension relative to the string's gauge and pitch. A balanced set has a tension curve that accounts for this — typically the bass strings sit slightly higher in tension than the treble strings, and the curve is smooth rather than jagged.

String manufacturers design their sets around this principle. A typical electric set might have tensions like this on a 25.5" scale:

Notice the curve: it dips slightly at the B string, then climbs toward the low E. That is intentional — it matches how tension feels across the set. A set where the low E pulls 16 lbs and the high E pulls 18 lbs would feel backwards.

Heavy-bottom sets

A "heavy-bottom" set (e.g. 10–52 or 11–49) puts more tension on the bass strings while keeping the treble strings light. This is popular for:

  • Drop tunings — the heavier bass strings keep tension up when you drop the low string.
  • Rhythm players who want a tight, punchy low end without stiff trebles for bending.
  • Baritone and multiscale instruments where the bass strings need extra tension to speak clearly.

The trade-off is that the tension curve becomes steeper — the low strings pull noticeably harder than the high strings. That can be exactly what you want, or it can make the set feel unbalanced if overdone.

The visual tension curve

StringSelectorApp plots tension as a curve across the strings, so you can see the balance at a glance. A well-balanced set shows a smooth, gently sloping line. A poorly matched set shows spikes and dips — a string that is way too tight or too loose stands out immediately.

How to read the curve: A flat line means every string has the same tension (rarely what you want). A smooth upward slope from treble to bass is typical and comfortable. A jagged line with a spike means one string is mismatched — swap it for a different gauge from a single-string pack, or choose a different set.

Truss Rod and Neck Relief

The truss rod is the steel rod inside the neck that counteracts string tension. Understanding how it works is the difference between a setup that lasts and one that fights you every time the weather changes.

How string tension affects the neck

A guitar neck is a lever. The strings pull the headstock forward (toward the bridge), and that force tries to bend the neck into a forward bow — creating relief (a slight concave curve). Without counterforce, the neck would bow so much the strings would sit miles above the frets.

The amount of bow depends on:

  • Total string tension — more strings, heavier gauges, higher tunings, or longer scales all increase the pull.
  • Neck stiffness — a thicker or multi-piece neck (maple/rosewood, mahogany/maple) resists bowing more than a thin one-piece neck.
  • Truss rod tension — the rod pulls back against the strings. Tightening it (clockwise) counteracts the bow; loosening it (counter-clockwise) lets the neck bow more.

Why total tension matters

When you change string sets or tunings, the total tension on the neck changes — and the truss rod setting that was perfect for the old set is now wrong for the new one. A rough guide:

ChangeEffect on total tensionTypical truss rod response
Go up a gauge (e.g. 10s → 11s)Increase (~10–15%)Tighten (clockwise) 1/8 to 1/4 turn
Go down a gauge (11s → 10s)DecreaseLoosen (counter-clockwise) 1/8 to 1/4 turn
Tune down a whole stepDecrease (~12%)Loosen slightly, or leave and accept more relief
Tune up a half-stepIncrease (~6%)Tighten slightly
Switch to a longer scaleIncrease (~6–10%)Tighten
Add a string (6 → 7)Increase (significant)Tighten, possibly more than 1/4 turn
Never force the truss rod. If it will not turn smoothly, stop. A broken truss rod is a very expensive repair. When in doubt, take it to a qualified tech. The app gives you an estimate — it is a starting point, not a substitute for a careful setup on the instrument in front of you.

How the app estimates truss rod adjustment

StringSelectorApp compares the total tension of your current setup against a reference (the tension the neck was set up for). The difference, combined with the neck's stiffness profile, gives an estimated number of truss rod turns. The report presents this as a recommendation — "tighten approximately 1/8 turn" — that you then verify by measuring relief with a feeler gauge or straightedge.

The 0.010" relief rule: A common starting point for electric guitar is 0.010" of relief at the 7th fret (measured with a feeler gauge while holding the low E at the 1st and 15th frets). This gives a tiny bit of forward bow for clean string vibration without fret buzz. Adjust to taste and playing style — some players want 0.005" (flatter, faster), others want 0.015" (more bow, lighter touch).

The Workshop Report

The workshop report is StringSelectorApp's deliverable — a printable HTML document that captures everything about a setup so you can archive it, hand it to a client, or compare it against a future setup.

What the report includes

Instrument & Setup Header

Instrument name, tuning, scale length, string pack, date, and technician. Everything you need to identify the job at a glance.

Tension Table

Every string with its note, gauge, unit weight, frequency, and tension in both pounds and kilograms (or Newtons in ISO mode). The raw data behind the setup.

Visual Tension Curve

A graph of tension across the strings, so you (and the client) can see the balance at a glance. Spikes and dips are immediately visible.

Truss Rod Recommendation

Total tension vs. reference, with an estimated truss rod turn count. The starting point for neck adjustment — not a substitute for measuring relief.

Notes & Observations

A free-text section for anything the numbers do not capture: fret wear, neck feel, player feedback, humidity, anything that explains the setup.

Print-Ready Layout

Clean HTML that prints to a single page (or two) on standard paper. Hand it to the client, file it, or email it as a PDF.

Why to archive setups

A guitar is a dynamic system. Neck relief changes with humidity, temperature, and string age. Fret wear changes action. Players change tunings. Without a record, you are guessing every time the guitar comes back. With an archived report, you can see exactly what the setup was last time — and what changed.

  • Repeat setups with confidence. When a client returns, pull up the last report and you know the starting point.
  • Diagnose changes. If the guitar now buzzes where it did not before, compare the tension curve — a new string set or a humidity shift will show up.
  • Client trust. A printed report shows the client you measured, calculated, and documented — not just eyeballed it.
  • Warranty & liability. If a neck warps or a truss rod breaks, the report documents what tension the instrument was subjected to and what you recommended.

How to use it with clients

  1. Run the calculation with the client's instrument, tuning, and string choice in front of you.
  2. Review the tension curve together. If a string looks mismatched, discuss alternatives before installing.
  3. Generate the report and print it (or save as PDF).
  4. Walk the client through it. Show them the total tension, the truss rod recommendation, and the balance curve. Most players have never seen this information — it is a value-add that sets you apart.
  5. File a copy in your records and give a copy to the client. Note the date and any follow-up work needed (e.g. "recheck relief after 48 hours").
Pro tip: Generate the report before you touch the truss rod. The report captures the "before" state. After adjustment, measure relief, generate a second report, and you have a before/after record that documents exactly what changed and why.
The report is a record, not a prescription. The tension numbers are calculated from published unit weights and standard formulas. Real necks vary. Always verify relief by measurement on the actual instrument, and use the truss rod estimate as a starting point — never as a mandate.
