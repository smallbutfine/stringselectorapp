1. What is StringSelectorApp?

StringSelectorApp is a string tension calculator and workshop reporter for guitars and basses. You give it an instrument, a tuning, a scale length, and a set of strings — and it tells you the exact tension of every string, ranks the best-matching string packs from its database, and produces a printable report you can hand to a client or file for later.

It is not a string-ordering tool or a brand endorsement. It is a measurement and documentation tool. The value is in knowing the real numbers behind a setup, so your decisions come from data instead of habit or guesswork.

Who it is for: Luthiers and guitar techs who set up instruments professionally, builders who need to balance multiscale and baritone instruments, and players who want to understand their own gear. If you touch a truss rod, this tool is for you.

2. The Physics of String Tension

Tension is the pulling force a tuned string exerts on the instrument. Every string is stretched between the bridge and the nut, and the amount of stretch is the pitch you hear. More pull means a higher note and a tighter feel. The whole instrument — especially the neck — is engineered around the total of these forces.

StringSelectorApp computes tension from a single equation, the ideal flexible-string formula solved for pounds-force:

T = (W × (2 × L × f)2) / 386.4
  • T — tension in pounds.
  • W — unit weight of the string (lbs per inch).
  • L — scale length (the vibrating length, in inches).
  • f — frequency of the note, in Hz.
  • 386.4 — the gravitational constant that converts the result to pounds-force.

You never do this math by hand — the app does it instantly. But understanding the four inputs lets you predict the result before you buy a single string.

3. Unit Weight: The Number That Matters

The single most important input is unit weight — how much the string weighs per inch. It is not the gauge. Two strings of identical gauge (say both .010") can differ by 20% or more in tension because of material (plain steel, nickel-plated, stainless, bronze), winding (roundwound, flatwound, tapewound), and core shape.

This is why StringSelectorApp stores the measured or published unit weight for every string in every pack, rather than guessing from gauge. When you add your own strings to strings.ini, use the manufacturer's published unit weight or measure it yourself: cut a 10-inch length, weigh it in grams on a precision scale, and convert with W (lbs/in) = grams ÷ 453.592 ÷ 10.

Never estimate unit weight from gauge alone. A .010" plain steel string and a .010" nickel-wound string are not interchangeable in tension. Trust the measured number, not the label on the packet.

4. Scale Length and Why It Changes Everything

Scale length is the vibrating length of the string — bridge to nut. It is the second biggest factor in tension, and because tension scales with the square of scale length, small differences matter. The classic Fender 25.5" and Gibson 24.75" differ by about 6% in tension from the formula, and closer to 8–10% in feel.

For multiscale (fanned-fret) and baritone instruments, each string has a different scale length. StringSelectorApp interpolates linearly between the treble and bass scale you enter, so the low strings get the extra length they need to balance against the high strings. Always enter the correct treble and bass scale for the instrument on your bench.

5. Tuning, Pitch, and Transposition

The app converts note names to frequencies using equal temperament, built on A4 = 440 Hz (adjustable 390–460 Hz for period or orchestral work). The Transpose field shifts the whole tuning by a fixed interval — handy for checking Eb, D, or Drop tunings without editing presets.

Tension follows frequency. Tuning up raises both frequency and tension; tuning down lowers both. A whole-step drop reduces tension by roughly 12% across the set — which is exactly why drop-tuned instruments often need heavier strings to feel right.

6. Balanced Tension vs. Equal Tension

"Balanced" does not mean every string has the same number of pounds. Your fingers perceive tension relative to a string's gauge and pitch, so a well-balanced set has a smooth tension curve — typically the bass strings sit slightly higher than the trebles. A set where the low E pulls 16 lbs and the high E pulls 18 lbs would feel backwards.

StringSelectorApp draws this curve for you. A smooth, gently rising line from treble to bass is the comfortable, professional target. A jagged line with a spike means one string is mismatched — swap it from a single-string pack or choose a different set. "Heavy-bottom" sets (10–52, 11–49) deliberately steepen the curve for drop tunings and a tight low end.

7. Truss Rod and Neck Relief

The truss rod is the steel rod inside the neck that counteracts string tension. The strings pull the headstock forward, creating relief (a slight forward bow); the rod pulls back. When you change sets or tunings, the total tension changes, and the truss rod setting that was perfect for the old setup is now wrong for the new one.

StringSelectorApp compares your total tension against a reference and estimates the truss rod turns needed — "tighten about 1/8 turn," for example. Treat this as a starting point, then verify by measuring relief: a common electric guitar target is 0.010" at the 7th fret, measured with a feeler gauge while holding the low E at the 1st and 15th frets.

Never force the truss rod. If it does not turn smoothly, stop — a broken truss rod is an expensive repair. The app's estimate is a mathematical starting point, not a substitute for careful measurement and experience on the instrument in front of you.

8. The Workshop Report

The workshop report is the deliverable — a printable HTML document that captures the entire setup: instrument and tuning header, a full tension table (note, gauge, unit weight, frequency, tension in lbs and kg/N), the visual tension curve, the truss rod recommendation, and a free-text notes section.

Archive every report. A guitar is a dynamic system — relief shifts with humidity and string age, frets wear, players change tunings. With an archived report you can repeat a setup exactly, diagnose what changed when a guitar comes back buzzing, and show the client documented proof of your work. Generate the report before you touch the truss rod to capture the "before" state, then again after, for a clean before/after record.

9. The Professional Workflow

  1. Talk to the player. Ask about tuning, style, and any feel complaints. A player who says the low end is "floppy" is describing a tension problem you can measure.
  2. Select the instrument and tuning in the app, or choose Custom and enter the notes yourself.
  3. Enter the correct scale — treble and bass. For multiscale instruments this is critical.
  4. Calculate and read the top recommendation and the tension curve.
  5. Discuss alternatives with the player if a string looks mismatched, before installing anything.
  6. Generate the report, then do the mechanical work (string change, truss rod, intonation).
  7. Re-measure and file the final report alongside the before report.
